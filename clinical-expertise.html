Mechanical ventilation & ARDS

When the lungs cannot maintain safe oxygen and carbon-dioxide levels, a ventilator takes over part of the work of breathing. The goal is not simply to push more air in, but to support the lungs in a way that lets them recover without being injured further — small, carefully limited breaths, controlled pressures, and close attention to oxygen levels.

In acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) the lungs become stiff and inflamed, often after severe infection, aspiration or major illness. Management may include lung-protective ventilator settings, positioning the patient face-down (proning) for part of the day, careful fluid balance, and treating the underlying cause. In the most severe cases, ECMO may be considered.

Advanced airway management

Securing and protecting the airway is often the first priority in a deteriorating patient. Dr. Roy's anaesthesiology training brings a practised approach to this — rapid-sequence intubation, planning for the anticipated and the unexpected difficult airway, video laryngoscopy and flexible bronchoscopy, and surgical front-of-neck access when it is needed.

In the ICU this also covers safe intubation of patients with very low oxygen levels or an unstable circulation, planned extubation, and tracheostomy care and decannulation as patients recover.

Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO)

ECMO is a form of life support for very severe heart or lung failure. Blood is drained from a large vein, passed through a machine that adds oxygen and removes carbon dioxide, and then returned to the body. It does not treat the underlying disease — it buys time for the heart or lungs to recover, or as a bridge to another treatment.

VV ECMO supports the lungs when ventilation alone is not enough, for example in severe ARDS. VA ECMO supports both the heart and lungs, for example in cardiogenic shock or after cardiac arrest in selected patients. ECMO is resource-intensive and carries real risks; the decision to start it is made carefully by the whole ICU team, weighing the likelihood of recovery against those risks.

Sepsis & septic shock

Sepsis is the body's overwhelming response to infection, in which the immune system's reaction begins to damage the patient's own organs. In septic shock, blood pressure falls to a dangerous level despite fluids, and organs such as the kidneys, liver and brain begin to struggle.

Early treatment matters: identifying and controlling the source of infection, timely antibiotics, intravenous fluids, and medicines (vasopressors) to restore blood pressure. Critical care adds continuous monitoring, support for organs that are failing, and repeated reassessment as the picture changes hour to hour.

CRRT & multi-organ support

When the kidneys fail in a critically ill patient, continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) does some of their work — removing waste products and excess fluid gently and continuously, which is better tolerated than standard dialysis when the circulation is unstable.

Critically ill patients often have several organs failing together. Care means coordinating support for each — lungs, heart, kidneys, liver, clotting, nutrition — while keeping sight of the whole person and the overall direction of the illness.

Point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS)

A portable ultrasound scanner used at the bedside lets the team answer urgent questions in minutes: is the heart filling and pumping well, is there fluid around the lungs or heart, is the patient dehydrated or overloaded, is a large vein safe to place a line into.

Used alongside the clinical examination and monitoring, focused ultrasound helps make treatment decisions more precise and reduces the need to move an unstable patient for other scans.

Neurocritical care

Some patients in intensive care have a primary problem with the brain — severe stroke, bleeding, infection, seizures that will not stop, or brain injury after trauma or cardiac arrest.

Care focuses on protecting the brain from further harm: maintaining oxygen and blood flow, controlling pressure inside the skull, managing temperature and seizures, and working closely with neurology and neurosurgery colleagues.

Referrals & second opinions

How to involve a critical care specialist

Referring clinicians and departments can make contact for advice on a deteriorating patient, ventilation strategy, or whether ECMO might be appropriate. Families can ask for a second opinion when a relative is in intensive care and they would value another perspective on the plan.
