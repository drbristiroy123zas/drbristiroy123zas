Understanding intensive care

If someone you care about is in the ICU, a lot of unfamiliar words arrive at once. These notes explain the common ones in plain language. They are general information, not advice about a specific patient — your treating team knows the details of the case.

What happens in the intensive care unit

The ICU is the part of the hospital for patients who need continuous monitoring and active support for one or more failing organs. Nurses look after a very small number of patients each, monitors track the heartbeat, blood pressure and oxygen level second by second, and a team reviews the plan several times a day.

Machines and lines can look alarming. Most are there to measure or to support — a breathing tube connected to a ventilator, fine tubes (lines) in a vein or artery for medicines and blood tests, a bladder catheter to measure urine, and sometimes a dialysis machine. Alarms are frequent and usually routine; staff respond to the ones that matter.

Sedation is often used so that a breathing tube is tolerated. Patients may be drowsy or not able to talk. Hearing is thought to be one of the last senses affected, so talking to your relative is worthwhile.

Mechanical ventilation (the "breathing machine")

A ventilator supports breathing when the lungs or the effort of breathing cannot keep up. Air with extra oxygen is delivered through a tube in the windpipe, usually placed through the mouth. If ventilation is needed for more than one to two weeks, a tracheostomy — a shorter tube through the front of the neck — is often more comfortable and easier to wean from.

"Weaning" is the gradual process of handing breathing back to the patient as they recover. It can take days or weeks and often moves forwards and backwards before it succeeds.

What is ECMO?

ECMO stands for extracorporeal membrane oxygenation. Blood is drained from a large vein through a tube (cannula), pumped through an artificial lung that adds oxygen and removes carbon dioxide, warmed, and returned to the body. It is used only for the most severe heart or lung failure, when a ventilator and medicines are not enough.

ECMO does not cure the underlying illness. It takes over the work of the heart or lungs so they can rest and, hopefully, recover — or so there is time to reach another treatment. It is demanding for the patient and the team, and it carries serious risks including bleeding, clots, stroke and infection. Because of this, the decision to start, continue or stop ECMO is made carefully and reviewed often, with the family kept informed.

Sepsis and septic shock

Sepsis is the body's extreme reaction to an infection, where the immune response starts to injure the body's own organs. Septic shock is sepsis with dangerously low blood pressure that does not recover with fluids alone.

Treatment includes finding and controlling the source of infection (sometimes with a procedure or surgery), antibiotics, intravenous fluids, and medicines called vasopressors to lift the blood pressure. Some organs may need temporary support — for example dialysis for the kidneys — while the infection is brought under control. Recovery can be slow, and tiredness and weakness for weeks or months afterwards is common.

ARDS (acute respiratory distress syndrome)

ARDS is severe, sudden inflammation of the lungs. The lung's air sacs fill with fluid, so oxygen cannot pass into the blood easily and the lungs become stiff. It is usually triggered by another serious problem such as pneumonia, severe infection elsewhere, or aspiration.

Management supports the lungs while the cause is treated: gentle ventilator settings to avoid further injury, nursing the patient face-down (proning) for part of the day, careful fluid balance, and — in the most severe cases — VV ECMO. Many people who survive ARDS recover good lung function, though it can take months.

Seeking a second opinion — what to prepare

It is reasonable to want another perspective when a relative is critically ill. A second opinion works best when it is based on complete information. If you are contacting Dr. Roy for this, it helps to have ready:

  • the hospital and unit where the patient is being treated, and the treating consultant's name;
  • a brief summary from the treating team of the diagnosis and current plan;
  • recent test results — blood tests, imaging reports, and any procedure or operation notes;
  • the current level of support (for example ventilator, dialysis, ECMO, and blood-pressure medicines);
  • a short written list of the questions that matter most to you.

A second opinion is advisory. It does not replace the treating team, who remain responsible for day-to-day decisions, and it is most useful when shared openly with them.

Common questions

Is being on a ventilator painful?

The breathing tube is uncomfortable, so sedation and pain relief are given to keep patients settled. Doses are adjusted so that patients are as comfortable and as awake as is safe.

Does ECMO mean the situation is hopeless?

No. ECMO is offered because the team believes there is a realistic chance of recovery or of reaching another treatment. It is a serious step, and the outlook depends on the underlying illness, which the treating team can discuss for a specific patient.

Why does my relative seem worse some days than others?

Critical illness rarely improves in a straight line. Day-to-day ups and downs are expected; the team looks at the trend over several days rather than any single day.

Can referring doctors or departments ask for input?

Yes. Clinicians and departments are welcome to use the contact form for advice on a deteriorating patient, ventilation strategy, or ECMO candidacy.

Does this website give emergency medical help?

No. For anything urgent, contact the hospital emergency department or your local emergency service. Messages sent here are not monitored around the clock.

Short glossary

Intubation
Placing a tube into the windpipe to connect a patient to a ventilator.
Extubation
Removing the breathing tube when a patient can breathe adequately on their own.
Tracheostomy
A short breathing tube placed through the front of the neck, used when ventilation is needed for longer.
Vasopressor
A medicine given through a drip to raise blood pressure when it is dangerously low.
Inotrope
A medicine that helps the heart pump more strongly.
CRRT
Continuous renal replacement therapy — a gentle, continuous form of dialysis used in unstable patients.
Proning
Nursing a patient face-down for part of the day to improve oxygen levels in severe lung failure.
Sedation hold
Briefly reducing sedation to check a patient's neurological state and readiness to breathe on their own.
Cannula (ECMO)
A large tube placed in a major blood vessel to carry blood to and from the ECMO circuit.
